Top High-Quality Built-in Materials
MeshPhysicalMaterial: Extends MeshStandardMaterial to provide advanced PBR rendering, including clearcoat (for varnished surfaces), anisotropy (for brushed metal), and sheen.
MeshStandardMaterial: The standard choice for PBR, utilizing metalness and roughness workflows for realistic, light-reactive surfaces.

MeshToonMaterial: Useful for stylized, cartoon-style shading that still reacts to lights. 

High-Quality Texture Maps 
Textures are applied to materials to add fine detail. The most common high-quality maps include: 
Normal Map: Simulates high-resolution, 3D surface details (bumps, wrinkles) without adding to the actual geometry vertex count.
Roughness Map: A grayscale map determining how rough or smooth a surface is, affecting how light scatters.
Metalness Map: A grayscale map defining which parts of the surface are metallic.
AO (Ambient Occlusion) Map: Simulates soft shadows in creases, corners, and crevices for added depth.
Displacement Map: Actually alters the geometry's vertex structure to create true, high-detail physical depth.
Alpha Map: A grayscale map that controls transparency, useful for complex surface cutouts.

**Anti‑tiling techniques**

- **Triplanar or tri-planar projection**: Instead of relying purely on baked UVs, project the plaster texture along each world axis and blend them. That “wraps” the texture around corners without obvious seams and is a common trick for architectural materials. In Three.js you can implement it in a shader (e.g., a custom node or `ShaderMaterial`) or via a custom `onBeforeCompile` helper that samples the same texture from three directions and blends by the surface normal.  
- **Detail/overlay textures**: Keep the high‑res plaster base, then add a second detail normal or albedo tile with a very fine grain (often 2K → 512 px) that repeats faster and multiplies/adds subtle noise. Because it’s blended at a higher frequency, the repeating pattern in the main plaster map becomes less noticeable.  
- **Masking & variation**: Combine several textures with mask maps or vertex colors so each wall can sample different sections of a texture atlas. You can mix the same plaster map with slightly shifted UV offsets or blend between two variants (e.g., `mix(texture1, texture2, noise)`) to hide repetition.  
- **Use decals or procedural noise**: Apply random decals (e.g., stains, plaster cracks) via extra UV layers or projected textures, and overlay subtle procedural noise (like a perlin map) in the fragment shader to break up regularity.  
- **Smart UV scaling**: Your current `RoomMeshCSG` scales UVs with a fixed repeat (`uvScale = 0.1`). Adjust the UV scale to match wall size, and combine that with triplanar mapping or detail textures so big surfaces don’t over-repeat one tile.  
- **Transition to PBR detail maps**: Keep the plaster at 4K for the base color but feed roughness/normal/occlusion variations from smaller detail maps that tile independently. This lets you maintain resolution while minimizing the perception of repeating patterns.

If you want to go further, you can add a small shader helper to blend between the painted plaster map and a noise texture, or adopt a technique like “texture atlasing with random UV offsets per wall” to give each surface a slightly unique part of the image.
